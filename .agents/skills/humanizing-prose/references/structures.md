# Structural templates

Reader-first structures replace the AI default of `intro → importance → benefits → challenges → conclusion` symmetric blocks. Pick one based on the piece's purpose, and apply it in Phase 4 Layer 1.

## The four templates

### Problem-first

Best for consulting, strategy, B2B essays.

1. Name the real problem (concrete, not abstract).
2. Explain why common thinking about it is wrong.
3. Show what actually works.
4. Give a practical next step.

Example opener: *"Most AI pilots don't fail because the model is weak. They fail because nobody changed the workflow around the model."*

### Claim-first

Best for executive writing, opinion pieces, LinkedIn-style posts.

1. Strong thesis stated immediately.
2. Evidence supporting it.
3. Implication of the thesis.
4. Counterpoint or concession.
5. Takeaway.

Example opener: *"AI strategy is not a technology plan. It is a decision-rights redesign."*

### Story-first

Best for personal essays, thought leadership, case studies, founder narratives.

1. Situation — set the scene specifically.
2. Friction — what went wrong, what didn't fit.
3. Decision — what was chosen, by whom.
4. Result — what happened.
5. Lesson — what generalizes.

Example opener: *"A client once asked us to automate customer support. After two workshops, we found the real issue: support was cleaning up pricing mistakes created upstream by sales."*

### Inverted pyramid

Best for web content, documentation, memos, product pages, anything readers will scan rather than read.

1. Main answer first.
2. Important details.
3. Supporting context.
4. Edge cases.

This structure is the basis of news writing and is well-suited to readers who may stop after the first paragraph. The Nielsen Norman Group's research on web reading patterns supports this layout for any content meant to be read on screen.

## Examples-before-explanations inversion

A separate structural move available within any of the above templates.

**AI default:** Define a concept, then give an example.

> *"Governance refers to the boundaries within which AI systems operate. For instance, companies should establish governance mechanisms to ensure responsible deployment."*

**Human inversion:** Lead with the concrete example, then name the concept.

> *"Before giving a sales team an AI email assistant, decide what it is not allowed to promise. Discounts? Delivery dates? Legal terms? That boundary is governance."*

The inversion makes the abstract concept feel like the natural label for an experience the reader has just had, rather than a definition they must accept on authority. Apply it to at least one paragraph per piece when the structural rewrite happens.

## Opener taxonomy

A working first line belongs to one of these types. Any other type is ornamental and should be cut or replaced.

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Contrarian | "Your AI roadmap is probably too technical." |
| Practical | "Start with the workflow, not the model." |
| Diagnostic | "If adoption drops after week two, you don't have a training problem. You have a relevance problem." |
| Story | "The client wanted a chatbot. The real problem was their pricing data." |
| Warning | "AI will not fix unclear ownership. It will expose it faster." |

Common ornamental openers to flag and replace:
- *"In today's rapidly evolving business landscape..."*
- *"In an era of unprecedented technological change..."*
- *"As AI continues to reshape industries..."*
- *"The world of [X] is undergoing a transformation..."*

## Ending taxonomy

A working ending does one of three things. Anything else is ceremonial.

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Next action | "Pick one workflow, define the decision it should improve, assign an owner, and measure whether the work actually gets faster." |
| Sharpen the claim | "AI does not create accountability. It rewards the teams that already have it." |
| Memorable distinction | "The model was accurate. The team still ignored it. That is where most AI programs break: not at prediction, but at adoption." |

Common ceremonial endings to flag and replace:
- *"In conclusion, AI offers transformative opportunities..."*
- *"Ultimately, the future of [X] depends on..."*
- *"By embracing these strategies, organizations can..."*
- *"As we move forward in this rapidly evolving landscape..."*

## Passage-priority weighting

Within any structure, three passages carry the highest fingerprint density and should receive the heaviest rewrite effort:

1. **Opener** — sets the entire piece's perceived authorship within the first 1–2 sentences.
2. **Transitions** between major sections — AI's mechanical connectors cluster here.
3. **Conclusion** — AI's summative clichés cluster here.

When time or token budget is constrained, prioritize these three. A piece with a strong working opener, logic-bearing transitions, and a non-ceremonial ending will read substantially more human even if the body paragraphs receive lighter editing.

## Choosing the template

| If the piece's purpose is to... | Use |
|---|---|
| Diagnose and prescribe a fix | Problem-first |
| Stake out a position and defend it | Claim-first |
| Share an experience that generalizes | Story-first |
| Inform a scanner who may not finish | Inverted pyramid |

When the user hasn't specified, infer from the existing draft's apparent purpose. When unclear, ask in Phase 1 alongside the voice-profile questions.
